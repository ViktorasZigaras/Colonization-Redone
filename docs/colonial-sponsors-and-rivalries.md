# Colonial sponsors and contested regions, 1500–1800

For a hemisphere-wide historical map, use five recurring powers. Spain and Portugal have the earliest large commitments. England, France, and the Dutch Republic become stronger competitors as their Atlantic networks develop. Entry dates below are suitable scenario anchors. They describe historical developments and need not gate a sandbox start.

| Power | Sponsors and instruments | Main historical footholds |
|---|---|---|
| Spain | Crown licenses and expeditions, royal officials and viceroyalties, missions | Caribbean from the 1490s, Mexico and Central America, the Andes, Florida and the Gulf, later the Pacific frontier |
| Portugal | Crown, hereditary captaincies, royal administration | Brazil, especially the Atlantic sugar coast |
| England, then Great Britain | Crown charters, Virginia Company, Massachusetts Bay Company, proprietors, Hudson's Bay Company | Chesapeake, New England, Atlantic seaboard, Caribbean, Hudson Bay |
| France | Crown and chartered companies, including the Company of New France and French West Indies Company | St. Lawrence, Acadia, Great Lakes and Mississippi trade routes, Caribbean |
| Dutch Republic | Dutch West India Company and merchants | Hudson and Delaware valleys, Caribbean trading ports, Suriname, temporary rule in northeastern Brazil |

## Where to put the competition

| Region | Rivals and period | What they competed over |
|---|---|---|
| Caribbean and its approaches | Spain, England, France, and the Dutch from the sixteenth and especially the seventeenth century. Denmark-Norway became a smaller participant. | Sugar islands, sheltered ports, shipping lanes, and access to Spanish markets and treasure routes. Plantation economies relied heavily on enslaved labor. |
| Northeastern Brazil | Portugal and the Dutch West India Company, especially 1630–1654 | Pernambuco sugar production, Recife, and Atlantic shipping |
| Hudson and Delaware | Dutch, Swedish, and English, chiefly mid-seventeenth century | River mouths, fur trading, and port access. New Sweden lasted from 1638 to Dutch conquest in 1655. England took New Netherland in 1664, with a brief Dutch return in the 1670s. |
| Acadia, St. Lawrence approaches, Hudson Bay, Great Lakes and Ohio Valley | France and England/Britain, especially from the late seventeenth century through 1763 | Fisheries and ports in the northeast, fur routes and Indigenous alliances farther inland, river access and forts in the Ohio Valley |
| Florida, Gulf and lower Mississippi | Spain, France, and England/Britain, with different neighbors and phases | Forts and river mouths, coastal shipping, the Carolina–Florida frontier and French Louisiana's Gulf access |
| Alaska and the Pacific Northwest | Russia enters in the late eighteenth century. Britain and Spain contest Pacific claims and access farther south. British and American merchants also compete with Russia in the maritime fur trade. | Sea otter trade, anchorages, and commercial access. Russian-American Company charter in 1799. |

## Suggested roster

Add Portugal first. Give it an early Brazil foothold and a distinct relationship with Dutch commercial expansion. Use Sweden for a Delaware scenario, Denmark-Norway for a Caribbean scenario, and Russia for a late Pacific scenario. Chartered companies can work as sponsors within their parent power, with different objectives and resources. Give the rival AI regional goals such as a harbor, a trade route, or a river corridor so historical starts create different decisions on each map.

Indigenous nations controlled territory, traded and negotiated on their own terms, and shaped which colonial ventures could operate. These overlap zones describe European competition within existing political and trade networks.
